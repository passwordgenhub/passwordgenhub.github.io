        How to Create a Strong Password

        A strong password is not a clever password. The ones that fall first in real breaches are rarely short of symbols or capital letters — they are short, reused, or built from things an attacker can guess: a pet's name, a birthday, a favourite team with a "1" stuck on the end. This guide explains what actually makes a password hard to break, and how to create one you can rely on.

        Length beats tricks

        Every extra character multiplies the work an attacker must do. A password of 8 characters, even with symbols mixed in, can be tested by modern hardware billions of times per second. At 16 characters, the number of possible combinations grows so large that brute force stops being a realistic option. That is why our generator starts at 16 characters: length is the cheapest security you can buy.

        Complexity rules — one capital, one number, one symbol — mostly push people toward predictable patterns like capitalising the first letter and ending with "!". Attackers know these patterns and try them first. A longer password chosen at random beats a shorter "complex" one every time.

        Randomness is the whole point

        Humans are terrible at being random. Asked to invent a password, we reach for words we like, dates we remember, and keyboard shapes that feel clever. Attack software is built on enormous lists of exactly those choices, gathered from past breaches. A generator that uses a cryptographically secure random source — as this site does, via your browser's Web Crypto API — has no favourites and no habits, so there is no pattern to exploit.

        Two good recipes

        1. A random password, stored in a manager

        For almost every account, generate 16–20 random characters with all character types switched on, and save it in a password manager. You never need to see it, type it, or remember it — the manager does that. This is the strongest option and the easiest to live with.

        2. A passphrase, for the few you must remember

        For your password manager's master password, your computer login, and anything else typed from memory, use a passphrase: five or six unrelated words chosen at random, like Harbour-Cactus-Velvet-Anvil-Tulip. The words make it memorable; the randomness and length make it strong. Adding a capital letter pattern or a trailing number helps a little, but the words do the heavy lifting.

        The one rule that matters most: never reuse a password. When a site is breached, attackers immediately try the leaked email-and-password pairs on banks, email accounts and shops — a technique called credential stuffing. A unique password per account confines a breach to that one account.

        What to avoid

        • Personal details. Names, birthdays, anniversaries, pets and places are the first things tried, and much of it is public on social media.
        • Dictionary words on their own. A single word, however long, appears in every cracking dictionary.
        • Predictable substitutions. Swapping "a" for "@" or "o" for "0" fools no modern tool; those variants are tried automatically.
        • Keyboard walks. Sequences like "qwerty", "1qaz2wsx" or "abcd1234" are in every attacker's starter list.
        • Small tweaks of an old password. Changing "Summer2024!" to "Summer2026!" does not protect you if the old one leaked.

        Finish the job: protect the account, not just the password

        Even a perfect password can be stolen by a phishing page or a breach. Turn on two-factor authentication wherever it is offered — an authenticator app is best, SMS is still better than nothing. Keep a unique email password above all: whoever controls your inbox can reset everything else. Finally, check whether your email has appeared in known breaches (your password manager or browser may warn you automatically), and change any reused password it flags.

        Password Security Tips

        Most account takeovers do not involve genius hacking. They involve a reused password from an old breach, a convincing phishing email, or an account with no second line of defence. These ten habits close off nearly all of it.

        1. Use a unique password for every account

        This is the single most valuable habit on the list. Sites get breached constantly, and the stolen passwords are tested against other services within hours. Unique passwords turn a breach at one site into a nuisance instead of a disaster.

        2. Let a password manager do the remembering

        Nobody can memorise fifty strong passwords, and nobody should try. A reputable password manager stores them all behind one strong master passphrase, fills them in for you, and warns you about weak or reused ones. Your only job is one good passphrase and your manager's two-factor setting.

        3. Turn on two-factor authentication (2FA)

        With 2FA, a stolen password alone is not enough to get in. Prefer an authenticator app or a hardware security key; use SMS codes if nothing else is offered — they still stop most opportunistic attacks.

        4. Make length your default

        When a site lets you choose, choose long: 16 characters or more for random passwords, five or six words for passphrases. Length is what pushes a password out of brute-force range.

        5. Guard your email account hardest

        Your inbox is the master key to your digital life, because password resets flow through it. Give it your strongest unique password and 2FA, and review its recovery options once a year.

        6. Learn to spot phishing

        Check the sender's actual address, not just the display name. Be suspicious of urgency — "your account will be closed in 24 hours" is a pressure tactic. When in doubt, do not click: open the site or app yourself and check there. A password manager helps here too, because it will not autofill your password on a look-alike domain.

        7. Keep software up to date

        Many attacks never touch your password at all — they exploit outdated browsers and operating systems. Turn on automatic updates for your devices, browser and password manager, and let them run.

        8. Be careful on public Wi-Fi and shared computers

        Avoid banking or email on shared computers entirely. On public Wi-Fi, a reputable VPN adds a layer of protection, and always check you are on the real site (https, correct domain) before signing in.

        9. Know what to do after a breach

        If a service announces a breach: change that password first, then change it anywhere you reused it (this is why tip 1 matters), turn on 2FA, and watch the account for unfamiliar activity. Speed matters more than perfection.

        10. Do not share passwords — and if you must, do it properly

        Never send passwords by email or chat, where they sit in archives forever. If a family member or colleague genuinely needs access, use your password manager's secure sharing feature, and revoke it when it is no longer needed.

        A quick self-check: if your email password is used anywhere else, or your bank login has no 2FA, fix those two things today. Then generate fresh passwords for the rest, one account at a time.

        Password Managers Compared: How to Choose the Right One

        Every security guide, including our own, eventually gives the same advice: use a password manager. But "use a password manager" is where the questions start, not where they end. There are dozens of them, they all promise to be secure, and their feature lists look nearly identical. This article explains the ways password managers genuinely differ, so you can choose based on the things that matter rather than the marketing.

        What every decent manager does

        Start with the baseline. Any reputable password manager stores your logins in an encrypted vault, protected by one master password — the only password you still have to remember. It generates random passwords for new accounts, fills them in on websites and apps, and syncs the vault across your devices. The encryption is done on your device before anything is synced, which means the company running the service cannot read your passwords even if it wanted to. If a manager cannot clearly explain that "zero-knowledge" design, cross it off the list.

        Cloud vaults vs local vaults

        The first real dividing line is where the encrypted vault lives. Cloud-based managers keep the encrypted file on the company's servers so it syncs automatically between your phone, laptop and tablet. This is the convenient option most people should choose: the vault is encrypted before it leaves your device, so the server only ever holds scrambled data. Local-only managers keep the vault on your own devices, and syncing (if you want it) is your problem to arrange. That suits people who are comfortable managing files and backups and who distrust any third-party storage, but it adds friction — and friction is the enemy of actually using the thing.

        There is also a middle path built into your devices already: the password managers bundled with browsers and operating systems have improved enormously and are a perfectly reasonable starting point. Their weakness is lock-in and thinner feature sets — fewer sharing options, less detailed security reporting — but a built-in manager used consistently beats a premium one abandoned after a week.

        The features that actually differentiate

        Passkey support. Passkeys are spreading quickly, and a good manager now stores and syncs them alongside passwords. If you are choosing today, pick a manager that handles passkeys well, or you will be migrating again in two years.

        Sharing. Families and small teams constantly need to hand a login to someone else. Managers differ a lot here: some offer proper shared vaults where an update reaches everyone, others only let you send one item at a time. If sharing matters to you, read how to share passwords safely and check the plan limits before paying.

        Security reporting. Most managers will audit your vault and flag weak, reused or breached passwords. The best reports prioritise well and make fixing things a one-click trip to the right site. This feature quietly does more for your security than almost anything else, because it finds the old, bad passwords you forgot existed.

        Emergency access and inheritance. What happens to your vault if you are incapacitated, or die? Some managers offer a designated emergency contact who can request access after a waiting period. It is an uncomfortable topic and an important one — a vault nobody can open is a vault full of locked doors.

        Independent audits. Reputable managers publish third-party security audits and penetration tests. You do not need to read them; you need the company to commission them and publish the summaries. Silence on this point is a red flag.

        Free vs paid

        Free tiers exist and some are genuinely good, but they usually limit syncing across device types or cap the number of stored items. Paid plans typically cost about the price of a coffee each month and add family sharing, larger storage for secure notes and documents, and priority support. For most households, a family plan shared between several people is the best value in all of consumer security: everyone gets unique passwords everywhere for a few dollars a month in total.

        A simple way to decide

        Answer three questions. First, which devices must it cover — if you mix phones, tablets and computers from different makers, choose a manager with apps for all of them. Second, who else needs access — just you, a partner, or a whole family? Third, how much hand-holding do you want — the built-in manager on your devices is the zero-effort option, while a dedicated manager rewards a little setup with better reporting and sharing. Whichever you choose, protect it properly: a long master passphrase built with a method from our guide to memorable passwords, plus two-factor authentication on the manager itself. The manager concentrates your security into one place; that place deserves the strongest lock you have.

        Two-Factor Authentication Explained: A Complete Beginner's Guide

        Two-factor authentication — usually shortened to 2FA — is the single biggest upgrade you can give an account after a unique password. It means that signing in requires two different kinds of proof: something you know (your password) and something you have (your phone, an app on it, or a small physical key). A criminal who steals or guesses your password still cannot get in, because they do not have the second thing. This guide explains how the different kinds of 2FA work, which are strongest, and how to set them up without locking yourself out.

        Why one factor is not enough

        Passwords leak. They leak in data breaches, they leak through phishing pages that look exactly like the real site, and they leak when people reuse them in the wrong place. None of that means passwords are useless — it means a password should not be the only wall between a stranger and your email, your bank, or your photos. 2FA adds a second wall that is built from different material, so a failure of the first does not collapse the whole defence.

        The main kinds of second factor

        Authenticator apps

        An authenticator app on your phone generates a fresh six-digit code every 30 seconds, based on a secret shared with the service when you set it up. The code works offline, never travels over the phone network, and cannot be intercepted by someone who has cloned your SIM card. This is the option to choose for most accounts: free, widely supported, and strong. Several reputable authenticator apps exist, and many password managers include the same feature built in.

        Security keys

        A hardware security key is a small device you plug in or tap against your phone. Instead of typing a code, you touch the key and it cryptographically proves it is present. Security keys are the strongest common form of 2FA because they are bound to the real website's address — a phishing page on a look-alike domain gets nothing from them. They cost money and can be lost, which is why people who use them register two. For most people an authenticator app is enough; for journalists, executives, and anyone whose accounts are high-value targets, keys are worth it.

        SMS and email codes

        A code sent by text message is the weakest common second factor. Text messages can be intercepted, and attackers have persuaded phone companies to transfer victims' numbers to new SIM cards — a "SIM swap" — purely to receive these codes. Email codes have a different weakness: they are only as safe as the email account itself. None of this makes SMS useless. Against the everyday, automated attacks that hit ordinary accounts, SMS 2FA still blocks the overwhelming majority. The honest rule: use an app or key where offered, use SMS where that is all there is, and never let "SMS is imperfect" talk you out of having any second factor at all.

        Passkeys: the factor that replaces the password

        Passkeys fold both factors into one gesture: your device holds a private key (something you have) and unlocks it with your face, fingerprint or PIN (something you are or know). Where a service offers passkeys, enabling one usually gives you stronger protection than a password plus a code, with less effort. Expect them to gradually become the default sign-in method over the next few years.

        Setting up 2FA without locking yourself out

        The fear that stops many people is losing the phone and being locked out forever. Services plan for this, and so should you. When you enable 2FA, most services offer a set of one-time recovery codes: save them. The safest home for them is inside your password manager as a note on the account, or printed and stored somewhere sensible at home — not in a photo on the same phone. In your authenticator app, turn on its encrypted backup option if it has one, so a new phone can restore your codes. Then protect the recovery path: your email account, which resets everything else, should have the strongest password you own and 2FA of its own.

        Which accounts to protect first

        You do not need to do everything in one evening. Work down this order: your email account first, because it can reset all the others; then banking and finance; then your password manager and your Apple or Google account, since those unlock your devices and stored passwords; then social media, which attackers use to impersonate you to your friends; then shopping and everything else as you go. Each one takes about two minutes once you know where the setting lives — usually under "Security" in the account settings. Fifteen minutes of setup removes the most common way accounts are stolen, and that trade is available to everyone.

        Passkeys Explained: The Future Beyond Passwords

        Passkeys are the first serious attempt to retire the password altogether. Instead of a secret you invent, remember and type, a passkey is a pair of cryptographic keys: one stays locked on your device, the other sits on the website's server. Signing in becomes a tap and a glance at your phone — and, crucially, there is no shared secret for anyone to steal. This article explains how passkeys work in plain language, why security experts are so enthusiastic about them, and where their limits still are.

        How a passkey actually works

        When you create a passkey for a site, your device generates two mathematically linked keys. The public key is sent to the website and stored there, much like a username. The private key never leaves your device (or your synced keychain or password manager). When you sign in, the website sends your device a one-off challenge; your device proves it holds the private key by signing that challenge, after you unlock it with your face, fingerprint or device PIN. The website checks the signature against the public key and lets you in.

        Three consequences fall out of this design. First, there is nothing to remember and nothing to type, so there is nothing to mistype or reuse. Second, the website never holds anything that can be used to impersonate you — a stolen database of public keys is useless to attackers, unlike a stolen database of password hashes. Third, each passkey is created for one specific website and will only work there.

        Why passkeys defeat phishing

        Phishing works because humans are bad at checking addresses: a page that looks like your bank, at an address that is one letter different, happily accepts the password you type into it. Passkeys remove the human from that check. The passkey is bound to the exact domain it was created for, and your device performs the matching automatically. On a fake domain, there is simply no passkey available to use — the sign-in cannot proceed, even if you are completely fooled by the page in front of you. This is the property that code-based two-factor authentication lacks: a code can be typed into a fake page, but a passkey cannot be used on one at all.

        Where your passkeys live

        Passkeys sync through the account you already use for your devices — your Apple ID keychain, your Google account, or a password manager that supports them — so a passkey created on your phone is available on your laptop too. That syncing is protected by the same end-to-end encryption as the rest of the keychain. Some high-security setups instead keep a passkey on a single hardware security key that never syncs; that is the strictest option, at the cost of convenience if the key is lost.

        The honest limitations

        Passkeys are not magic, and it helps to know the edges. Not every site offers them yet, and support quality varies — some sites still fall back to a password for account recovery, which means the password's weaknesses remain in the chain. Your security now rests heavily on your device account: someone who controls your Apple or Google account and your devices has a path to your passkeys, so those accounts deserve your strongest protection and two-factor settings. And if you lose access to every device and your synced account at once, recovery depends on the fallback methods each service offers, which can be slow. None of these are reasons to avoid passkeys; they are reasons to set them up thoughtfully rather than assuming the job is finished.

        What to do now

        When a site you use offers to create a passkey, accept — it takes seconds and replaces your most phishable credential with your least. Prioritise your email, banking and primary device accounts. Keep your passwords where passkeys are not yet offered, generated and stored as usual; the two systems will coexist for years. The password is not dead, but for the first time there is a mainstream successor that is both easier and safer — and that combination is why this one is likely to stick.

        How Long Does It Take to Crack a Password?

        "How long would it take to crack this password?" sounds like a question with one answer. In reality the answer swings from "instantly" to "longer than the age of the universe" depending on three things: how the password was chosen, how long it is, and — the factor most people forget — how the website stored it. This article walks through the realistic numbers so you can judge your own passwords with clear eyes.

        First, which kind of attack?

        Crack-time figures usually assume an offline attack: the attacker has stolen a site's password database and can test guesses on their own hardware as fast as it can run, with no rate limits and no lockouts. That is the worst case, and the honest one to plan around, because databases are stolen regularly. In an online attack — typing guesses into a login page — the site's defences slow things to a crawl, which is why brute force is mainly an offline threat. All the times below assume a well-equipped offline attacker testing billions of guesses per second against a weakly protected database.

        The passwords that fall instantly

        Any password that has appeared in a previous breach is, for practical purposes, already cracked: attackers keep combined lists of billions of real passwords and try them before anything else. The same goes for dictionary words, names, keyboard walks and predictable patterns like a word plus a year. These are not "cracked" by computation at all — they are simply looked up. This is why our strength checker treats common passwords as near-zero strength regardless of how they look, and why the first rule of password safety is not cleverness but never reusing a password that exists anywhere else.

        Random passwords: length does the work

        For a truly random password, the maths is clean: the attacker must search a space of possibilities that grows with every character. Against fast hardware testing around ten billion guesses a second, an 8-character password using letters, digits and symbols (roughly 52 bits of entropy) falls in minutes to hours. Add two characters — 10 characters, about 65 bits — and the same attack stretches to decades. At 12 characters it reaches hundreds of thousands of years, and at 16 characters the expected time exceeds the current age of the universe by an absurd margin. These are averages for searching half the space, and hardware improves, but the shape of the curve is the point: each character multiplies the work by the size of the alphabet, so modest length increases move you from "minutes" to "effectively never".

        Passphrases: words multiply too

        A passphrase of random words follows the same logic with a bigger unit. Each word picked truly at random from a list of a few thousand adds roughly 12 bits. Four words give about 50 bits — respectable against casual attacks but within reach of a determined offline effort. Five words reach the low 60s, comparable to a strong random password, and six words push past 75 bits, comfortably out of range. The catch is the word "random": words you choose yourself because they feel meaningful are far more predictable than words a generator picks, which is why passphrases should come from a generator like the one on our home page, not from your imagination.

        The storage wildcard

        Every figure above can shift by a factor of billions depending on how the website stored its passwords. Sites that use modern, deliberately slow hashing algorithms — covered in how password hashing and salting work — can reduce an attacker's speed from billions of guesses per second to mere thousands, turning "minutes" into "centuries" for the same password. Sites that used fast, obsolete hashing hand attackers the worst case. You cannot control which kind of site you are dealing with, and breaches rarely announce it politely. The rational response is to assume the worst case and choose passwords that survive it: 16 random characters, or a five-to-six-word passphrase, unique to each account. Do that, and the honest answer to "how long to crack it?" becomes "longer than anyone will ever spend trying".

        Password Entropy Explained: Measuring Real Strength

        Ask ten password checkers about the same password and you may get ten different verdicts, because many of them grade on appearances — a symbol here, a capital there. Entropy is the attempt to do better: a single number, measured in bits, that estimates how many guesses an attacker would need. Understanding entropy will not just help you read our strength checker; it will change how you think about every password you make.

        What a "bit" of entropy means

        Entropy counts uncertainty. One bit of entropy means the attacker faces two equally likely possibilities — like guessing a coin flip. Two bits mean four possibilities, three bits mean eight, and every additional bit doubles the space the attacker must search. A password with 40 bits of entropy has about a trillion possible values; at 60 bits it is about a quadrillion; at 80 bits, more than a trillion trillion. Because the growth is exponential, small differences in bits are enormous differences in safety: a 60-bit password is not half again as strong as a 40-bit one, it is roughly a million times harder to guess.

        How entropy is calculated for random passwords

        For a password generated truly at random, the maths is exact: entropy equals the length multiplied by the logarithm (base 2) of the alphabet size. Each lowercase letter contributes about 4.7 bits, each character from the full set of letters, digits and symbols about 6.6 bits, and each random word from a large word list about 12–13 bits. So a 16-character random password from the full set carries roughly 105 bits, and a five-word passphrase around 64 bits. This is the number our generator displays, and for generated passwords it is a guarantee, not a guess — provided the randomness is real, which is why generators should use a cryptographically secure random source rather than anything a human or a weak algorithm produces.

        Why human passwords have far less entropy than they look

        The formula above assumes every character is chosen independently and uniformly. Human choices violate both assumptions spectacularly. When people "add a symbol", they overwhelmingly add "!" at the end; when they capitalise, it is the first letter; the "random" word is a name, a place, a team, a season. Attackers do not guess character by character — they guess the way humans construct, trying billions of real passwords from past breaches, then systematic variations of them. Measured honestly, a typical human-invented "complex" password like a word with substitutions and a year often carries only 20–30 bits of effective entropy, which is why such passwords fall in seconds despite passing every composition rule. Our guide to common password mistakes is essentially a catalogue of ways humans drain entropy out of their passwords.

        How many bits do you need?

        It depends on the attack you are defending against. Against online guessing — an attacker typing at a login page that rate-limits attempts — even 40 bits is ample. Against offline cracking of a stolen database with fast hardware, aim higher: around 60 bits is a sensible floor for important accounts, 80 bits is strong, and anything past 100 bits is beyond any plausible brute-force effort, as the crack-time estimates show. Well-stored passwords (protected by slow hashing, as explained in hashing and salting) need fewer bits for the same safety, but since you cannot verify how each site stores yours, planning for the harsher case is the prudent default.

        Using entropy day to day

        Entropy gives you a practical shopping rule: buy bits with length, not with contortions. Adding two characters to a random password adds about 13 bits — more than any substitution trick has ever added to a human password — and costs nothing when a manager remembers it. For the passwords you must remember, buy bits with words: each extra random word in a passphrase adds a solid, honest chunk. Judge every password by that one question — how many bits, really? — and most password advice becomes obvious.

        The Most Common Password Mistakes (and How to Fix Them)

        Almost nobody sets out to have a bad password. Bad passwords happen through small, understandable habits — habits attackers understand even better, because they build their tools around them. Here are the mistakes that show up again and again in breached data, why each one is dangerous, and the specific fix for each.

        Mistake 1: Reusing the same password everywhere

        This is the champion of password mistakes, and it is not close. When any one site is breached, the leaked passwords are immediately tested against email, banking and shopping accounts in automated credential stuffing attacks. Your careful, strong password on your bank's site does nothing if the identical password was sitting in a breached forum database. The fix: a unique password per account, which in practice means using a password manager — nobody can memorise fifty unique passwords, and nobody should have to.

        Mistake 2: Building passwords from personal information

        Children's names, pets, birthdays, anniversaries, favourite teams: these are the first entries in every targeted guessing list, and most of them are public on social media or inferable from it. Even combined — a pet's name plus a birth year — the search space is tiny. The fix: passwords should have no relationship to your life at all. Generate them, or build passphrases from random words that mean nothing to you.

        Mistake 3: Predictable patterns and substitutions

        Capitalise the first letter, swap "a" for "@", end with "!" or the current year: these transformations feel like strengthening, but cracking tools apply exactly these rules automatically to every dictionary word. "P@ssword2026!" passes most complexity checkers and falls almost instantly. The fix: get your strength from length and genuine randomness instead — see how entropy actually works — rather than from decorations an attacker expects.

        Mistake 4: Tweaking an old password instead of replacing it

        Changing "Spring2025!" to "Spring2026!" after a breach, or when a site forces a change, leaves you almost exactly as exposed as before: anyone holding the old password will try the obvious increments. The fix: when a password needs changing, generate a completely new one with no lineage from the old. Our guide to how often you should change passwords explains when changes are truly needed.

        Mistake 5: Writing passwords down in unsafe places

        The sticky note on the monitor is the cliché, but the modern versions are worse: passwords in an unencrypted notes app, a spreadsheet called "passwords.xlsx", or emails to yourself — all readable by anyone who gets into the device or account. A nuance matters here: a paper list kept in a genuinely secure place at home, like a locked drawer, is a far better option than reusing weak passwords, especially for people who are not comfortable with technology. The fix: a password manager is the proper home; paper in a locked place is an acceptable backup, never the monitor, the desk drawer at work, or a synced plain-text file.

        Mistake 6: Sharing passwords over chat and email

        A password pasted into a chat thread lives there forever — searchable, screenshot-able, and exposed if either account is ever compromised. The fix: share through a password manager's sharing feature, which keeps the secret encrypted and revocable; see how to share passwords safely.

        Mistake 7: Ignoring the second factor

        A strong password with no two-factor authentication is one phishing page or one breach away from being public knowledge. People skip 2FA because setup feels fiddly; it takes about two minutes per account and neutralises most stolen-password attacks outright. The fix: enable it on email, banking and your password manager today, and the rest as you go.

        The pattern behind the mistakes

        Every mistake on this list comes from the same root: human memory is the bottleneck, so people optimise passwords for remembering instead of for resisting attack. The fixes all point the same way too — let machines do the remembering. Generate unique passwords, store them in a manager, add a second factor, and the entire list of mistakes becomes mostly irrelevant to you.

        How to Create a Password Policy for Your Business

        A password policy is only worth having if people can actually follow it. The history of corporate password rules is a cautionary tale: policies that demanded a new password every 30 days, with three character classes and no repeats, produced exactly what you would expect — passwords like "January2026!" written on notes under keyboards. Modern guidance from standards bodies has reversed much of the old advice. This article lays out a policy that reflects how attacks actually work and how humans actually behave.

        Start with the manager, not the rules

        The single most effective clause in any modern policy is providing a business password manager to every employee and requiring its use for work accounts. This one decision removes the memory problem that causes reuse and weak choices, gives you a way to share credentials properly (see sharing passwords safely), and — critically — lets you revoke access the day someone leaves. Individual accounts for every person, never shared logins for a whole team, should be a hard rule: shared accounts destroy accountability and can never be properly taken back.

        Length over complexity

        Set a minimum length — 12 characters at least, 14 or more is better — and drop composition rules like "must contain a symbol". Composition rules push people toward the same predictable constructions, while length delivers real entropy. Allow every character type including spaces so passphrases work, allow pasting (blocking paste breaks password managers, a mistake some systems still make), and set a generous maximum length so generated passwords are never truncated.

        Screen new passwords against breach lists

        The most valuable technical control available today is checking a proposed password against lists of passwords known from previous breaches, and rejecting matches. An employee who independently chooses the same password they use elsewhere — or one millions of people have used — is handing attackers a working key, and no length rule catches it. Many identity systems and password managers can perform this screening automatically. Pair it with the education in our credential stuffing explainer so staff understand why their favourite password was refused.

        Require multi-factor authentication, sensibly

        Multi-factor authentication should be mandatory for email, remote access, finance systems and administrator accounts at a minimum, and ideally everywhere it is supported. Authenticator apps are the practical default; hardware security keys for administrators and finance staff are worth their modest cost. Our beginner's guide to two-factor authentication can serve as the staff handout for setup day. Plan the rollout with recovery in mind: a documented, identity-checked process for the inevitable lost phone, so staff are never tempted to share accounts "just for today" while locked out.

        Change on evidence, not on a calendar

        Do not force routine password expiry. Research and current standards guidance agree that scheduled rotation produces weaker, predictable variations and endless reset-desk calls, without stopping real attacks. Require immediate changes on evidence instead: a breach notification involving your systems or a service staff use, a suspected phishing success, a lost device, or a departure from the team. Our article on how often passwords should change covers the reasoning you can cite to leadership.

        Cover the edges: admins, service accounts, and joiners and leavers

        Administrator and service accounts deserve their own stricter section: longer generated passwords or keys, stored in the company vault, never known by heart, and reviewed whenever anyone with access changes roles. Write the joiner and leaver process into the policy itself — accounts created before day one, access removed the day someone leaves, shared vaults transferred to a named owner. Most real-world incidents trace back to a departed employee's still-active account or a shared password nobody remembered to rotate, not to exotic hacking.

        Write it so people can follow it

        Keep the policy short — two pages people read beat twenty they skim. State the small number of rules plainly, explain the reason for each in one line, and provide the tools before enforcing the rules. Then audit gently: most business managers can report on weak or reused passwords across the company without seeing the passwords themselves. Treat what you find as a training list, not a disciplinary one. A policy that respects how people work gets followed; one that fights them gets circumvented, usually invisibly.

        Memorable Password Methods: Passphrases, Patterns and Mnemonics

        A handful of passwords still have to live in your head: your password manager's master password, your computer login, your phone's backup PIN, maybe your email. For those, memorability is a genuine requirement — but it does not have to mean weakness. This article compares the main methods people use to make passwords memorable, separating the ones that preserve real strength from the ones that only feel clever.

        The passphrase method (the one to use)

        Pick four to six words completely at random and string them together: lantern-otter-census-plum-waffle. The strength comes from the randomness and the count — each word chosen from a list of a few thousand adds about 12 bits of entropy, so five words land around 60 bits, and six pass 70. The memorability comes free: the human brain is far better at holding a string of concrete words, especially once you picture the silly scene they make, than at holding nine random characters. This approach is sometimes called Diceware, after the method of rolling dice to pick words from a printed list; our passphrase generator does the same job with secure randomness. Two rules make or break it: the words must be chosen by a random process, not by you picking favourites, and you should use at least five words for anything important.

        The sentence method

        A close cousin: invent a full sentence you can picture — "The giraffe borrowed my umbrella on Tuesday" — and use it whole, spaces included, where the system allows spaces. A genuinely original sentence has decent length and is easy to recall. Its weakness is that human sentences follow grammar and common word patterns, so its real entropy is lower than a random word string of the same length. Used for a device login it is acceptable; for a master password, prefer random words. Never use a famous quote, a song lyric or a line from a film: those are all in cracking dictionaries, word for word.

        The first-letter mnemonic

        An older technique takes the first letters of a sentence: "My first dog was called Biscuit and he loved sausages" becomes "MfdwcBahls". This produces something that looks random and is genuinely memorable. Its honest weakness is length: a comfortable sentence gives you perhaps 10–12 characters of moderately unpredictable text, which lands in the same territory as a modest random password — fine for a low-value login, short of what a master password deserves. If you use this method, use a long, personal, unpublished sentence, and add words or a number you can also remember to push the length up.

        Methods that feel smart but are not

        Keyboard patterns — walking shapes across the keys — are among the first things cracking software tries, in every direction. Substitution ciphers on a meaningful word (a becomes @, o becomes 0) add almost nothing, because the substitutions are standardised and applied automatically to every wordlist; see common password mistakes for the full story. And any method that produces the same structure for every site — a base word plus the site's name, for instance — collapses the moment one site's password leaks, because the pattern is visible in it. A memorable method must still produce a unique password per account; that constraint is exactly why memorable methods are for the few passwords in your head, and a manager handles the rest.

        Making the memory stick

        Whatever method you choose, the memory is built by use, not by study. Type the new master passphrase deliberately a few times on the first day, use it daily, and keep a sealed paper copy somewhere safe at home for the first few weeks until it is burned in — paper in a locked drawer is a reasonable temporary scaffold, as discussed in our seniors' guide. Within a fortnight, a five-word passphrase feels as natural as your own name — while remaining, to any attacker, one of quadrillions of possibilities.

        What Is a Brute Force Attack?

        A brute force attack is the simplest idea in hacking: try passwords until one works. No trickery, no cleverness — just guesses, in enormous numbers, made by machines. It sounds primitive, and against strong passwords it is hopeless. Against weak ones it remains devastatingly effective. Understanding how brute force works explains most of the password advice you have ever been given.

        Online brute force: guessing at the door

        In an online attack, the guesses are typed into the real login page. This is slow and noisy: each attempt travels over the network, and any competently run service notices thousands of failed logins, slows them down, demands extra checks, or locks the account. That is why online brute force usually targets breadth instead of depth — attackers try a handful of the most common passwords against millions of accounts rather than millions of passwords against one account, a pattern sometimes called password spraying. The defence on your side is simply not to have one of the common passwords, and to have two-factor authentication so that even a correct guess is not enough.

        Offline brute force: guessing in private

        The dangerous version happens after a database is stolen. The attacker copies the password data to their own machines and guesses at whatever speed their hardware allows — no lockouts, no rate limits, no witnesses. Modern graphics cards test billions of simple password hashes per second, and purpose-built rigs multiply that further. This is the scenario behind the crack-time estimates elsewhere on this site, and it is why the quality of a site's password storage, covered in hashing and salting, matters so much.

        Brute force is rarely purely blind

        Pure brute force — trying "aaa", then "aab", then "aac" — is the last resort, not the first. Real attacks work through a smart order: passwords from previous breaches first, then dictionary words, then words with the standard transformations (capitalised, a digit or symbol appended, common substitutions), then patterns and dates, and only then systematic guessing. This hybrid approach is why human-chosen passwords fall so much faster than their length suggests: they are not being searched for blindly, they are being predicted. Measured in entropy, a password's defence is not how long it looks, but how far down that ordered list it sits.

        Why length defeats it

        Brute force has one fatal weakness: arithmetic. Every additional character multiplies the possibilities by the size of the alphabet — by about 95 for the full character set. Going from 8 to 12 characters multiplies the attacker's work by roughly 95 four times over, about eighty billion times more work. There is no cleverness that escapes multiplication; there is only the question of whether the total exceeds the attacker's budget of time and hardware. At 16 random characters, it exceeds any budget that exists, which is why our generator defaults to that length.

        The defences, on both sides

        Services defend with rate limiting, lockouts and slow password hashing, and by screening new passwords against known-breached lists. You defend with the things entirely in your control: long, random, unique passwords from a generator; a password manager so uniqueness is effortless; and a second factor so that a guessed password is a dead end. Brute force is a solved problem for anyone who does those three things — it remains a threat only to passwords that were chosen to be memorable shortcuts, which is precisely the habit this site exists to replace.

        What Is Credential Stuffing — and Why Reused Passwords Feed It

        Credential stuffing is the attack behind a large share of the "someone logged into my account" stories you hear — and it involves no password cracking at all. Attackers take email addresses and passwords leaked from one breached website and automatically try them, in bulk, on other websites. Because so many people reuse the same password everywhere, a meaningful fraction of those tries simply work. This article explains the mechanics, why the attack is so hard for websites to stop, and the two habits that make it useless against you.

        Where the credentials come from

        Every year, breaches at companies large and small spill databases containing millions or billions of account records. Those collections are traded, merged and refined into enormous combo lists — email and password pairs, cleaned and deduplicated. The lists are not secret or rare; they circulate widely among criminals, and checking tools confirm which pairs still work. Your password does not need to be weak to end up on such a list. It only needs to have been used at a site that got breached — which, over a decade of online life, describes nearly everyone.

        How the attack runs

        Credential stuffing is fully automated. Software loads a combo list and a target site, then attempts logins at scale, rotating through networks of compromised computers and rented proxies so the attempts look like ordinary traffic from ordinary places. Success rates are low in percentage terms — often well under one in a hundred — but the lists are so large and the attempts so cheap that the yield is thousands of working accounts per run. Those accounts are then exploited according to their type: email accounts are mined for password resets and personal data, shopping accounts for stored cards and loyalty points, streaming accounts for resale, and social accounts for impersonation and scams against the victim's contacts.

        Why websites struggle to stop it

        The uncomfortable truth is that a credential stuffing attempt uses the correct password. From the website's side, it looks almost identical to you logging in from a new city. Sites fight back with bot detection, device fingerprinting, login rate analysis and challenges, and they win much of the time — but it is an arms race against attackers who only need a small success rate. Some sites now also screen their own users' passwords against breach corpora and force resets when a match is found, a practice we recommend in our business policy guide. Even so, no website can fully protect an account whose password is public knowledge elsewhere.

        Your two defences, both decisive

        The first defence is uniqueness. A unique password per account confines any breach to that one account: the pair that leaks simply does not fit anywhere else, and the stuffing run gets nothing from you. This is the practical reason uniqueness outranks almost every other password rule, and it is only achievable at scale with a password manager. The second defence is two-factor authentication: even when a stuffed credential is correct, the attacker lacks your second factor and the login fails. Either defence alone defeats the attack; together they make your accounts close to worthless targets for it.

        Checking your own exposure

        You can find out whether your email address appears in known breaches using the methods in our breach-checking guide. If it does, do not panic — an appearance means the pair is circulating, not that anyone has used it against you. It does mean the password from that era should be treated as burned: anywhere you still use it, or anything resembling it, gets a fresh generated password and a second factor. Credential stuffing thrives on old, reused passwords. Starve it, and it moves on to easier lists.

        How Password Hashing and Salting Work

        When you create an account, the website needs a way to check your password later without keeping a copy of it lying around for thieves. The standard solution — hashing, strengthened by salting — is one of the most elegant ideas in everyday security. You do not need mathematics to follow it, and understanding it will help you judge the breach headlines you read, because the difference between a bad breach and a catastrophic one is usually how the passwords were stored.

        Hashing: a one-way fingerprint

        A hash function takes your password and produces a fixed-length string of characters — its fingerprint. The function is deterministic (the same password always gives the same fingerprint, so the site can check your login by comparing fingerprints) and one-way (the fingerprint cannot be reversed back into the password). When you sign in, the site hashes what you typed and compares it with the stored fingerprint; your actual password need never be stored at all. If the database is stolen, the thieves get fingerprints, not passwords.

        The catch is that fingerprints can be attacked by guessing: hash a candidate password, compare, repeat. Modern hardware does this billions of times per second against fast hash functions — which is why plain fast hashing, designed for speed, is exactly wrong for passwords. That problem is what salting and slow hashing solve.

        Salting: making every password unique to crack

        A salt is a random value, different for every user, that is mixed with the password before hashing and stored alongside the fingerprint. Salting defeats two attacks at once. First, it kills precomputation: without salts, an attacker can hash a billion common passwords once and compare the results against every stolen database ever taken; with salts, that work must be repeated from scratch for every single user. Second, it hides sameness — two users with the same password have different fingerprints, so the attacker cannot even see which accounts share passwords. A breach report that says "passwords were salted and hashed" is describing a meaningfully better situation than one that does not, though as you are about to see, the details matter.

        Slow hashing: bcrypt, Argon2 and friends

        Password-specific hash functions — bcrypt, scrypt, and the current recommendation Argon2 — are deliberately slow and expensive to compute, with a cost that can be tuned as hardware improves. A good setup might take a tenth of a second to verify one login: imperceptible to you signing in, but devastating to an attacker, because it caps guessing at a handful of attempts per second per machine instead of billions. The difference between a fast hash and a slow, salted one can be the difference between every password in a breach being recovered within days and only the weakest few ever falling. This is the storage wildcard behind the wide ranges in our crack-time article.

        What this means for you

        You cannot inspect how each site stores your password, and most never say. So draw the practical conclusions. First, assume any breached password protected only by fast hashing is effectively public — if you get a breach notification, change that password and anywhere it was reused, as described in our breach guide. Second, prefer services that are transparent about using modern hashing; it is a good proxy for their overall security culture. Third, remember the asymmetry that protects you: strong storage defends weak-ish passwords poorly and strong passwords superbly. A 16-character random password behind even mediocre hashing is safer than a clever 8-character one behind the best. Storage is the site's half of the bargain; length and uniqueness are yours.

        How to Check If Your Password Has Been Leaked in a Data Breach

        Billions of passwords from breached websites circulate among criminals, feeding attacks like credential stuffing. The good news is that you can check your own exposure — safely — and that finding yourself in a breach is a fixable problem, not a catastrophe. This guide explains how breach checking works, how to do it without creating new risks, and exactly what to do with the answer.

        How breach checking can be safe at all

        It is reasonable to ask: isn't typing my email or password into a "checker" itself dangerous? For reputable services, the design answers that. Email-based checkers simply look up whether your address appears in their collection of breached data — you are giving them an address, not a password. Password-based checkers use a cleverer trick called k-anonymity: your browser hashes the password locally and sends only the first few characters of that hash. The service returns every known hash beginning with those characters (usually hundreds), and your browser checks the full match locally. Your password, and even its full hash, never leaves your device. Many password managers and browsers now build this same check in and run it across your whole vault automatically, which is the most convenient option of all — see password managers compared.

        Checking, step by step

        Start with your email addresses — most people have two or three, including an old one they rarely use. Enter each into a well-known breach notification service and note which breaches each appears in and roughly when. Then run a password check: your password manager's built-in audit is best, because it tests the passwords you actually use today, including the ones you have forgotten about, and flags the reused ones at the same time. If you do not use a manager yet, checking your two or three most important passwords individually through a k-anonymity checker is a reasonable substitute. One firm rule: only use checkers you reached by typing a well-known service's address yourself or through your manager or browser. Never follow a link from an alarming email that says "your password has been leaked, click here to check" — that is a phishing template, not a warning.

        What the results mean

        An appearance in a breach means that at some point, an account registered with that email address was in data that escaped. It does not prove anyone has used it against you, and older breaches may involve passwords you have long since changed. Treat the result as a to-do list, not a verdict. If a specific breached service is named and you still have an account there with the same password, that account is the priority. If your current passwords check out clean and are unique per site, an old breach listing may require no action at all.

        What to do if you find a match

        Work in this order. First, change the password on the breached service itself, to a fresh generated one. Second, change the password anywhere else you used the same one — or a close variation, because attackers try variations too, as our mistakes guide explains. Third, turn on two-factor authentication on those accounts, so even a password you missed becomes insufficient on its own. Fourth, scan the affected accounts for anything you do not recognise — sent emails, changed recovery addresses, unfamiliar orders — and check whether the breached data included financial details that need a word with your bank. Finally, make it the last time: unique generated passwords everywhere mean the next breach notice is an item to read, not a weekend to lose.

        Make checking a habit, lightly

        Register your email addresses with a breach notification service so new breaches come to you, enable your browser's and manager's built-in monitoring, and treat every notification with the same calm routine: change, check for reuse, add the second factor. Breaches are now a permanent feature of online life; the people they hurt are overwhelmingly the ones whose leaked password was also the key to five other doors.

        Password Security for Seniors: A Simple, Safe Setup

        If passwords feel like they have become unmanageable in recent years, that is because they have — for everyone. The average person is now expected to remember dozens of logins, and the rules keep changing. The good news is that a safe setup does not require technical skill or a great memory. It requires one tool, a short list of passwords that truly matter, and a couple of habits that block the scams aimed at older users. This guide walks through that setup in plain language, at a sensible pace.

        The one-tool solution: a password manager

        A password manager is a program that remembers all your passwords for you, locked behind one password that you choose. You sign into websites with a click instead of typing, and every site gets its own long, random password that you never need to know. The one password you do remember — the master password — should be a passphrase: four or five ordinary words strung together, like purple teapot garden whistle. Words are far easier to remember than jumbled characters, and a string of them is genuinely strong; our memorable password methods article explains why. If setting up a manager feels daunting, ask a trusted family member to sit with you for the first session — it is a one-time job of perhaps half an hour, and it ends the password struggle for good.

        The short list that matters most

        Not all accounts are equal. Four deserve your strongest protection. Your email comes first, because password resets for everything else are sent there — whoever controls your email can work their way into the rest. Next, your banking. Then the password manager itself. And finally the account for your phone or computer (your Apple or Google account), which can locate, lock or erase your devices. For these four, use the manager's strongest passwords, and add the extra step described next.

        The extra step: two-factor authentication

        Two-factor authentication means the website asks for a code from your phone as well as your password, so a stolen password alone is useless to a thief. It sounds complicated but in daily life it is a few seconds: a code appears, you type it in. Our beginner's guide to two-factor authentication explains the options with pictures-in-words. If the codes feel fiddly, ask about using your fingerprint or face to approve sign-ins instead — many services now allow that, and it is both easier and safer.

        About writing passwords down

        You may have heard that writing passwords on paper is always wrong. The honest advice is more practical. A notebook kept at home in a drawer, away from the computer, is far safer than using one weak password everywhere because remembering is impossible — a burglar is much less likely than an online criminal working from another continent. Never carry passwords in a wallet or purse, never tape them to the computer, and once a password manager is running, the notebook can be retired or kept only as an emergency backup. What matters is avoiding the real dangers: reuse, and passwords stored in the open.

        The scams to expect

        Most attacks on older people do not crack passwords at all — they ask for them. Be permanently suspicious of phone calls, emails or messages claiming to be from your bank, a computer company, or a government office, especially any that create urgency or ask you to read out a code, install software, or move money. Real banks do not phone to ask for passwords or codes. If a call worries you, hang up and dial the number on your bank card yourself. A password manager quietly helps here too: it will only fill your password on the genuine website, so if it refuses to fill on a page that looks like your bank's, treat that as a warning sign and stop.

        A calm summary

        One manager, one passphrase made of words, the extra code step on your four important accounts, and a standing rule to never give passwords or codes to anyone who contacts you. That is the whole system. It is simpler than what most people are struggling with now, and considerably safer.         Securing Your Home Wi-Fi: Router Passwords Done Right

        Your home router is the front door to everything on your network — computers, phones, televisions, cameras, doorbells — yet most routers are still running on the settings and passwords they left the factory with. Securing yours takes about fifteen minutes and two passwords. This guide explains both, plus the handful of settings that actually matter.

        Your router has two passwords, and they do different jobs

        The first is the Wi-Fi password (sometimes called the network key): the one you type into a phone or laptop to join your home network. The second is the admin password: the one that opens the router's own settings pages, where everything about your network can be changed. The two are often confused, and the second is the one most people never change — many routers ship with famous defaults like "admin" and "password", printed in manuals available to anyone. An attacker or a mischievous neighbour who reaches your settings page with a default admin password owns your network, whatever your Wi-Fi password is. Change both.

        Choosing the passwords

        For the Wi-Fi password, you want something long that guests can still type without misery. A passphrase of four or five random words is ideal — strong, and readable aloud across a room. Use our passphrase generator and save the result in your password manager. For the admin password, nobody ever needs to type it socially, so make it a fully random generated password of 16 characters or more, also stored in the manager. Do not use the same password for both, and do not reuse your router passwords anywhere else — the usual rules for strong passwords apply here in full.

        Getting into the settings

        Open a browser on a device already connected to your network and go to the router's address — commonly 192.168.0.1 or 192.168.1.1, and printed on the router's label if neither works. Sign in with the current admin details (also usually on the label if never changed), then look for the administration or system section to set a new admin password, and the wireless section for the Wi-Fi password and the settings below. While you are there, install any firmware update the router offers — updates patch the security holes that make headlines, and many routers can be set to update themselves automatically.

        The settings that matter

        Encryption: set your network to WPA3 if all your devices support it, or WPA2 (AES) as the fallback. Never WEP or the original WPA — both are broken beyond repair, and an open network with no password invites anyone in range onto your connection. WPS: turn off Wi-Fi Protected Setup; its PIN mode has a known design flaw that lets attackers recover your password by persistence. Remote management: make sure administration from the internet is off, so the settings page is reachable only from inside your home. Network name: you may rename your network to something that does not identify you or the router model — avoid your name, address or anything personal.

        Set up a guest network

        Most modern routers can broadcast a second, separate network for visitors. Use it. Guests get internet access without reaching your computers and storage, and — just as valuable — the growing crowd of smart-home gadgets, cheap cameras and other internet-of-things devices can live on the guest network too, isolated from the devices that hold your files and banking. When a visitor leaves, you can change the guest password freely without disconnecting everything you own. For sharing the main password with family in the meantime, the habits in sharing passwords safely apply to Wi-Fi codes as much as to logins.

        After you change the Wi-Fi password

        Expect a small parade of reconnections: phones, laptops, the television, the printer, anything smart. Work through them with the new passphrase in hand — it is a one-time chore, and the act of doing it is a useful census of what is actually on your network. If you find a device you do not recognise in the router's list of connected clients, that is worth investigating before you finish. A quarter of an hour, two strong passwords, and your home's front door is properly locked.

        Passwords for Kids and Family Accounts: A Parent's Guide

        Children now get their first accounts before they can ride a bike — school platforms, games, video apps, their first email. Each one needs a password, and each one is an early lesson, good or bad, in how to handle them. This guide offers an age-by-age approach that keeps children safe now and builds habits that will protect them as adults, without turning the family home into an IT department.

        Start with a family password manager

        The practical foundation is a family plan on a password manager. Each family member gets their own vault; parents get a shared family vault for the logins everyone legitimately uses — the household streaming service, the Wi-Fi code (see securing your home Wi-Fi), the smart TV. For children, the manager matters for two reasons: it generates proper random passwords for their accounts so they never develop the reuse habit, and it gives parents a recovery path when — not if — a password is forgotten on the morning of a school deadline. Set it up together, and let the child choose their own master passphrase from random words using a method like the ones in our memorable passwords guide: ownership makes habits stick.

        Ages roughly 6 to 10: parents hold the keys

        For younger children, keep it simple and supervised. Parents create the accounts, the manager holds the passwords, and children sign in with a parent's help or on devices that stay signed in. Use this stage to plant the two big ideas in child-sized language: a password is like the key to your bedroom — you do not give copies to friends — and you never type it into a page that pops up asking unexpectedly. Keep gaming and app purchases behind a parent's approval, since "free" games are where young children most often meet requests for credentials or payment details.

        Ages roughly 11 to 13: supervised independence

        Early secondary school brings personal email, messaging and social platforms with age limits that deserve respect rather than quiet circumvention. Children this age can run their own vault in the family manager, choose their own passphrases, and learn to recognise the classic traps: messages claiming their account will be deleted, strangers offering game currency for a login, fake "free download" pages. Agree as a family that parents can review the vault if something goes wrong — framed as a safety net, not surveillance — and that no password is ever shared with a friend, including best friends, including "just to check something". Friendships change; passwords given away cannot be ungiven, only changed.

        Ages 14 and up: real accounts, real rules

        Teenagers hold accounts that genuinely matter — email tied to exam boards and job applications, banking apps, social accounts with reputations attached. The parent's job shifts from holding keys to teaching the adult system: unique generated passwords everywhere, two-factor authentication on email and banking, and a healthy reflex about phishing and impersonation, which now often arrives as a message from a "friend" whose account was taken over. Talk through what to do when something goes wrong — a strange login alert, a friend reporting odd messages from their account — using the steps in our breach guide, so the first incident is a drill they have already rehearsed rather than a panic.

        Shared family accounts without the mess

        Households run on shared things: streaming profiles, grocery accounts, the family photo library, homework platforms. Keep those in the shared vault so every authorised person uses the same, current password, and never let a shared account reuse the password of anyone's personal account. When an older child leaves home, the shared vault makes the transition clean — remove them from items they no longer need, or move household items to a new arrangement — rather than the password living forever in a departed teenager's messages. Our article on sharing passwords safely covers the mechanics in detail.

        The lesson underneath

        Children copy what they see. If they watch you generate a fresh password for a new account and save it in the manager without thinking twice, that becomes their normal. If they watch you reuse the family dog's name everywhere, that becomes normal instead. The technology in this guide is simple; the real gift is making careful password habits unremarkable — just how things are done in your house.

        Biometrics vs Passwords: Which Is Safer?

        Your phone probably unlocks with your face or fingerprint, and it feels like the future: no typing, no forgetting, nothing to steal. So are biometrics simply better than passwords? The honest answer is that they solve different problems, each has failure modes the other lacks, and the strongest real-world setups use them together. This article compares them fairly.

        What biometrics actually do

        A biometric system measures something about your body — the ridges of a fingertip, the geometry of a face, the pattern of an iris or voice — and compares it with a stored reference. Two details matter. First, the comparison is probabilistic: the system accepts a close-enough match, with a tuned balance between letting you in reliably and keeping strangers out, unlike a password, which is exactly right or wrong. Second, on a well-built device the reference data never leaves a protected chip on the device itself; your fingerprint is not uploaded to a cloud database. What the biometric usually does on your phone is not replace the password — it unlocks the stored credentials, standing in for the PIN or passphrase you would otherwise type dozens of times a day.

        The case for biometrics

        Biometrics cannot be forgotten, lost in a notebook, reused across websites, or phished out of you by a convincing fake page — there is nothing to type into the fake page. They are also effortless, and effortlessness is a security feature in disguise: because unlocking is painless, people actually keep their devices locked, and they accept longer, stronger underlying passwords and PINs since they rarely have to type them. And a biometric is bound to your physical presence, which is exactly what remote attackers on another continent do not have.

        The case against relying on them alone

        You cannot change your face. A password that leaks can be replaced in a minute; a biometric template that leaks is compromised for every system that trusts it, for life — which is why on-device-only storage matters so much, and why centralised biometric databases are a genuinely worrying idea. Biometrics can also be compelled or applied without cooperation in ways a memorised password cannot: a phone can be held up to a face or pressed to a sleeping finger, and the legal protection for "something you are" is weaker than for "something you know" in many places. They fail in mundane ways too — wet fingers, injuries, ageing faces — so every biometric system has a fallback, and the fallback is usually a password or PIN. The strength of the whole arrangement is then the strength of that fallback.

        Passwords' side of the ledger

        Passwords are changeable, shareable in a controlled way (see sharing passwords safely), and work anywhere with no special hardware. Their famous weaknesses — guessable, reusable, phishable — are weaknesses of human-chosen passwords, largely cured by generating and managing them properly. Meanwhile the industry is merging the two ideas: a passkey is a cryptographic credential that lives on your device and is unlocked by your biometric, giving you a secret that is unphishable and unguessable, gated by something you are. That combination, not either technology alone, is the direction of travel.

        The verdict for everyday life

        Use biometrics to unlock your devices and approve sign-ins — they make strong security convenient, and convenience wins. Keep strong, unique passwords underneath, protected by your password manager, because they remain the fallback and the foundation. Add two-factor authentication where biometrics are not involved. And know how to switch your device to its PIN-only mode (most phones have a quick way to disable biometric unlock) for situations — borders, protests, a lost-and-found phone — where you want the lock to require something in your head rather than something on your face. Safer is not a technology; it is the combination, chosen deliberately.

        How Often Should You Change Your Password? What the Evidence Says

        For decades, the standard advice was to change every password every few months, and many workplaces enforced it automatically. Then the evidence came in, and the experts changed their minds: major standards bodies and national security agencies now advise against routine password changes. This article explains why the old advice backfired, when you genuinely should change a password immediately, and what to spend the effort on instead.

        Why scheduled changes made things worse

        The theory behind rotation was that it limited how long a stolen password remained useful. The practice, observed across millions of real users, was different. Faced with inventing yet another password every 30 or 90 days, people chose minimally different ones — incrementing a number, swapping a season, rotating a small set — and researchers showed that anyone holding a user's old password could predict the new one within a few guesses in a large share of cases. Rotation also pushed people toward weaker base passwords (complexity is easier to give up than memory), more reuse across systems on different schedules, and more passwords written down. Meanwhile the supposed benefit was mostly illusory: stolen credentials are typically used within hours or days, not politely held until the next quarter, so a 90-day rotation window stops almost nothing. The control consumed enormous effort and returned negative security.

        Change immediately when there is a reason

        The modern rule is event-driven. Change a password at once when any of these happen: the service announces a breach, or your details appear in one (our breach-checking guide shows how to find out); you receive a login alert you do not recognise, or spot activity that is not yours; you entered the password on a page you now suspect was phishing; you shared it with someone who no longer needs access — a departed employee, an ex-partner, a tradesperson; or you discover the password was reused on another account that has been compromised. In each case, the change should be to a completely new generated password, not a variation of the old one, for the reasons in common password mistakes.

        What to do instead of rotating

        The effort that rotation consumed buys far more security spent elsewhere. Make every password unique and generated, so a breach anywhere exposes nothing anywhere else — the defence against credential stuffing, the attack that actually uses stolen passwords at scale. Turn on two-factor authentication, which makes a stolen password insufficient whenever it was taken. Keep breach monitoring switched on in your password manager and browser so the "events" above reach you quickly. And let your manager's security audit, not the calendar, drive your maintenance: it will point at the genuinely weak, old or reused passwords worth changing today, which is a smarter list than "everything, again".

        What about workplaces that still force changes?

        If your employer or a service still imposes expiry, you have to live with it — but live with it well. Let your password manager generate each new password as a fully random one rather than a tweak of the last, and the forced change costs you nothing in strength. If you are the person who sets policy, our business password policy guide walks through replacing calendar rotation with breach screening and event-driven changes, with the reasoning leadership will ask for.

        The short answer

        A strong, unique password with two-factor authentication can stay unchanged for years in good conscience. A weak or reused one should be changed today, not at the next scheduled rotation. Change on evidence, not on anniversaries — and put the saved effort into uniqueness and second factors, where the evidence says the protection actually lives.

        How to Share Passwords Safely with Family and Coworkers

        "Never share your password" is correct as a default and impossible as a life rule. Families share streaming services, photo libraries and the Wi-Fi code; coworkers share the social media login, the analytics account, the supplier portal that only offers one username. The question is not whether sharing happens but whether it happens through channels designed for secrets — or through chat threads and emails where the password will live forever. This guide covers the safe mechanics.

        Why chat and email are the wrong channels

        A password sent by message or email is copied into inboxes, backups, notification previews and search indexes on at least two people's devices and several companies' servers. It stays there indefinitely, retrievable by anyone who later compromises any of those accounts, and it can be forwarded in one tap, deliberately or by accident. There is also no way back: you cannot unsend a secret someone has read, and you usually cannot even tell who has seen it. Every property you want from sharing — control, visibility, revocability — is absent.

        The right tool: your password manager's sharing

        Every major password manager offers sharing built for this. Typically you place the login in a shared vault or share the single item with another user of the same manager. The password travels end-to-end encrypted, appears in their vault, and — the crucial part — stays under your control: when the password changes, their copy updates; when access should end, you revoke it and rotate the password, and their access genuinely ends. Some managers also offer one-time sharing links that let you send a credential to someone without an account, expiring after a single view or a set number of hours — the correct replacement for the "I'll just text it to you" moment.

        Sharing at home

        For families, a shared family vault is the cleanest arrangement: household logins live there, everyone authorised uses the same current password, and personal accounts stay personal. Our family password guide covers the setup for children of different ages. Two household rules prevent most friction: shared accounts get their own unique passwords (never a copy of anyone's personal password), and when someone leaves the household arrangement — a grown child setting up independently, a lodger moving out — the shared passwords they knew get changed as a matter of routine, not suspicion.

        Sharing at work

        At work, the stakes include accountability: when five people know a password, no audit log can say who did what. Prefer individual accounts wherever a service offers them, even at extra cost. Where a genuinely shared login is unavoidable, keep it in the team's vault in the business manager, restrict the vault to the people who need it, and rotate the password whenever a team member with access leaves — build that step into the offboarding checklist, as described in our business policy guide. Never let shared work credentials be a variation of anyone's personal password, and never be the person who keeps the only copy in a spreadsheet.

        If you already shared the risky way

        Plenty of passwords are already sitting in old chat threads; treat this as a cleanup, not a scandal. Change the passwords that were shared insecurely, starting with anything financial or email-related, and re-share them properly through the manager. Deleting the old messages is a reasonable extra step but does not substitute for changing the password, since you cannot verify every copy is gone. From then on, the habit is simple: passwords travel only through the vault, and anything else — however urgent it feels — waits the extra thirty seconds to do it properly.

        Frequently Asked Questions

        Straight answers about how this generator works and how to keep your accounts safe.

        Is PasswordGenHub safe to use?

        Yes. Every password is generated locally in your browser using the Web Crypto API (crypto.getRandomValues), the standard source of secure randomness built into modern browsers. Nothing you generate or type is transmitted to a server or stored anywhere, so there is nothing for us — or anyone else — to leak.

        What makes a password strong?

        Length and unpredictability. A password of 16 or more characters drawn randomly from letters, numbers and symbols is extremely hard to crack. Uniqueness matters just as much: never reuse a password across accounts, because a breach at one site then exposes the others.

        How long should my password be?

        Use at least 12 characters for ordinary accounts and 16 or more for important ones such as email, banking and your password manager. Our generator defaults to 16 characters, and going to 20 costs you nothing when a manager remembers it for you.

        Are passphrases better than random passwords?

        Both can be excellent. A passphrase of five or six random words is long, memorable and high in entropy, which makes it ideal for a master password or anything you type by hand. Random character passwords are the better choice for accounts stored in a password manager, where memorability does not matter.

        What is entropy, and why does it matter?

        Entropy, measured in bits, describes how unpredictable a password is. Each extra bit doubles the number of guesses an attacker must try. Around 60 bits is a sensible minimum for important accounts; 80 bits or more is very strong, and a 16-character random password from this generator exceeds 100 bits.

        Should I use a password manager?

        Yes. A password manager lets you use a unique, random password for every account without having to remember them all. You only need to remember one strong master passphrase — which our passphrase generator is designed to create.

        How often should I change my passwords?

        Modern security guidance is to change a password when there is a reason — a breach, accidental sharing, or signs of compromise — rather than on a fixed schedule. Forced routine changes tend to produce weaker, predictable variations. Long, unique passwords do not need rotation.

        Does the strength checker send my password anywhere?

        No. The checker analyses your password entirely on your device. It is never sent over the network, never logged and never stored. You can verify this yourself: load this page, switch your device to flight mode, and the checker still works. Even so, we recommend testing a similar password rather than one you actively use.

        Why exclude ambiguous characters?

        Characters like 0 and O, or 1, l and I, look nearly identical in many fonts. Excluding them makes a password easier to read back or type by hand without mistakes. It slightly shrinks the character pool, but adding a character or two of length more than makes up for it.

        Can I generate several passwords at once?

        Yes. Set the quantity to anything from 1 to 10 and the generator produces a batch, each with its own copy button — handy when you are setting up several accounts or rotating a set of credentials. Everything you generate during a visit is listed in the session history on the home page, and it disappears when you close the tab.
