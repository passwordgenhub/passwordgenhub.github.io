        How to Create a Strong Password

        A strong password is not a clever password. The ones that fall first in real breaches are rarely short of symbols or capital letters — they are short, reused, or built from things an attacker can guess: a pet's name, a birthday, a favourite team with a "1" stuck on the end. This guide explains what actually makes a password hard to break, and how to create one you can rely on.

        Length beats tricks

        Every extra character multiplies the work an attacker must do. A password of 8 characters, even with symbols mixed in, can be tested by modern hardware billions of times per second. At 16 characters, the number of possible combinations grows so large that brute force stops being a realistic option. That is why our generator starts at 16 characters: length is the cheapest security you can buy.

        Complexity rules — one capital, one number, one symbol — mostly push people toward predictable patterns like capitalising the first letter and ending with "!". Attackers know these patterns and try them first. A longer password chosen at random beats a shorter "complex" one every time.

        Randomness is the whole point

        Humans are terrible at being random. Asked to invent a password, we reach for words we like, dates we remember, and keyboard shapes that feel clever. Attack software is built on enormous lists of exactly those choices, gathered from past breaches. A generator that uses a cryptographically secure random source — as this site does, via your browser's Web Crypto API — has no favourites and no habits, so there is no pattern to exploit.

        Two good recipes

        1. A random password, stored in a manager

        For almost every account, generate 16–20 random characters with all character types switched on, and save it in a password manager. You never need to see it, type it, or remember it — the manager does that. This is the strongest option and the easiest to live with.

        2. A passphrase, for the few you must remember

        For your password manager's master password, your computer login, and anything else typed from memory, use a passphrase: five or six unrelated words chosen at random, like Harbour-Cactus-Velvet-Anvil-Tulip. The words make it memorable; the randomness and length make it strong. Adding a capital letter pattern or a trailing number helps a little, but the words do the heavy lifting.

        The one rule that matters most: never reuse a password. When a site is breached, attackers immediately try the leaked email-and-password pairs on banks, email accounts and shops — a technique called credential stuffing. A unique password per account confines a breach to that one account.

        What to avoid

        • Personal details. Names, birthdays, anniversaries, pets and places are the first things tried, and much of it is public on social media.
        • Dictionary words on their own. A single word, however long, appears in every cracking dictionary.
        • Predictable substitutions. Swapping "a" for "@" or "o" for "0" fools no modern tool; those variants are tried automatically.
        • Keyboard walks. Sequences like "qwerty", "1qaz2wsx" or "abcd1234" are in every attacker's starter list.
        • Small tweaks of an old password. Changing "Summer2024!" to "Summer2026!" does not protect you if the old one leaked.

        Finish the job: protect the account, not just the password

        Even a perfect password can be stolen by a phishing page or a breach. Turn on two-factor authentication wherever it is offered — an authenticator app is best, SMS is still better than nothing. Keep a unique email password above all: whoever controls your inbox can reset everything else. Finally, check whether your email has appeared in known breaches (your password manager or browser may warn you automatically), and change any reused password it flags.

        Password Security Tips

        Most account takeovers do not involve genius hacking. They involve a reused password from an old breach, a convincing phishing email, or an account with no second line of defence. These ten habits close off nearly all of it.

        1. Use a unique password for every account

        This is the single most valuable habit on the list. Sites get breached constantly, and the stolen passwords are tested against other services within hours. Unique passwords turn a breach at one site into a nuisance instead of a disaster.

        2. Let a password manager do the remembering

        Nobody can memorise fifty strong passwords, and nobody should try. A reputable password manager stores them all behind one strong master passphrase, fills them in for you, and warns you about weak or reused ones. Your only job is one good passphrase and your manager's two-factor setting.

        3. Turn on two-factor authentication (2FA)

        With 2FA, a stolen password alone is not enough to get in. Prefer an authenticator app or a hardware security key; use SMS codes if nothing else is offered — they still stop most opportunistic attacks.

        4. Make length your default

        When a site lets you choose, choose long: 16 characters or more for random passwords, five or six words for passphrases. Length is what pushes a password out of brute-force range.

        5. Guard your email account hardest

        Your inbox is the master key to your digital life, because password resets flow through it. Give it your strongest unique password and 2FA, and review its recovery options once a year.

        6. Learn to spot phishing

        Check the sender's actual address, not just the display name. Be suspicious of urgency — "your account will be closed in 24 hours" is a pressure tactic. When in doubt, do not click: open the site or app yourself and check there. A password manager helps here too, because it will not autofill your password on a look-alike domain.

        7. Keep software up to date

        Many attacks never touch your password at all — they exploit outdated browsers and operating systems. Turn on automatic updates for your devices, browser and password manager, and let them run.

        8. Be careful on public Wi-Fi and shared computers

        Avoid banking or email on shared computers entirely. On public Wi-Fi, a reputable VPN adds a layer of protection, and always check you are on the real site (https, correct domain) before signing in.

        9. Know what to do after a breach

        If a service announces a breach: change that password first, then change it anywhere you reused it (this is why tip 1 matters), turn on 2FA, and watch the account for unfamiliar activity. Speed matters more than perfection.

        10. Do not share passwords — and if you must, do it properly

        Never send passwords by email or chat, where they sit in archives forever. If a family member or colleague genuinely needs access, use your password manager's secure sharing feature, and revoke it when it is no longer needed.

        A quick self-check: if your email password is used anywhere else, or your bank login has no 2FA, fix those two things today. Then generate fresh passwords for the rest, one account at a time.

        Frequently Asked Questions

        Straight answers about how this generator works and how to keep your accounts safe.

        Is PasswordGenHub safe to use?

        Yes. Every password is generated locally in your browser using the Web Crypto API (crypto.getRandomValues), the standard source of secure randomness built into modern browsers. Nothing you generate or type is transmitted to a server or stored anywhere, so there is nothing for us — or anyone else — to leak.

        What makes a password strong?

        Length and unpredictability. A password of 16 or more characters drawn randomly from letters, numbers and symbols is extremely hard to crack. Uniqueness matters just as much: never reuse a password across accounts, because a breach at one site then exposes the others.

        How long should my password be?

        Use at least 12 characters for ordinary accounts and 16 or more for important ones such as email, banking and your password manager. Our generator defaults to 16 characters, and going to 20 costs you nothing when a manager remembers it for you.

        Are passphrases better than random passwords?

        Both can be excellent. A passphrase of five or six random words is long, memorable and high in entropy, which makes it ideal for a master password or anything you type by hand. Random character passwords are the better choice for accounts stored in a password manager, where memorability does not matter.

        What is entropy, and why does it matter?

        Entropy, measured in bits, describes how unpredictable a password is. Each extra bit doubles the number of guesses an attacker must try. Around 60 bits is a sensible minimum for important accounts; 80 bits or more is very strong, and a 16-character random password from this generator exceeds 100 bits.

        Should I use a password manager?

        Yes. A password manager lets you use a unique, random password for every account without having to remember them all. You only need to remember one strong master passphrase — which our passphrase generator is designed to create.

        How often should I change my passwords?

        Modern security guidance is to change a password when there is a reason — a breach, accidental sharing, or signs of compromise — rather than on a fixed schedule. Forced routine changes tend to produce weaker, predictable variations. Long, unique passwords do not need rotation.

        Does the strength checker send my password anywhere?

        No. The checker analyses your password entirely on your device. It is never sent over the network, never logged and never stored. You can verify this yourself: load this page, switch your device to flight mode, and the checker still works. Even so, we recommend testing a similar password rather than one you actively use.

        Why exclude ambiguous characters?

        Characters like 0 and O, or 1, l and I, look nearly identical in many fonts. Excluding them makes a password easier to read back or type by hand without mistakes. It slightly shrinks the character pool, but adding a character or two of length more than makes up for it.

        Can I generate several passwords at once?

        Yes. Set the quantity to anything from 1 to 10 and the generator produces a batch, each with its own copy button — handy when you are setting up several accounts or rotating a set of credentials. Everything you generate during a visit is listed in the session history on the home page, and it disappears when you close the tab.
